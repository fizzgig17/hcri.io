Temp Logger (infrared)

Started November 1, 2026 · Raspberry Pi Pico · Download main.py · Web logger

01 What it is

Three non-contact thermometers (MLX90614, the GY-906 board) read the temperature of up to three spots on a light at once, for example the head, the body and the tailcap, once a second. A Raspberry Pi Pico reads them and sends the numbers over its USB cable. The logger on your computer records them, draws the chart, and can send them to hCRI.io to watch live, the same way the TKLamp logger works.

This page covers the hardware and the Pico firmware, which is the first step. The logger, the Companion app screen and the hCRI.io pages come next (see Status).

One sensor only? Skip the multiplexer for now: connect the sensor straight to the Pico (VIN to 3V3 OUT, GND to a GND pin, SDA to GP4, SCL to GP5). The firmware (2026.11.01.2 and later) notices there is no multiplexer and reads that sensor as sensor 1. When you add the multiplexer later, move the sensor to SD0/SC0; the firmware switches by itself and needs no change.

Keep the sensor cables short (under about 50 cm) and use the 3.3 V supply, not 5 V, for the sensors and multiplexer. The sensors are SMBus parts: the firmware runs the bus at 100 kHz. The GY-906 and multiplexer boards usually carry their own pull-up resistors, so no extra ones are needed.

04 Aiming lasers

Each sensor looks at a small spot. A laser module fixed beside it shows where. Wire each laser (red = +, blue = −) to the Pico's VBUS (5 V from the USB cable) through a slide switch, and to GND. They are not controlled by the firmware, so the switch is the only control.

Safety. 650 nm at 5 mW is a class 3R laser: never point it at a person or an animal, or at a shiny surface that could reflect it into an eye. Switch the lasers off for the measurement, since they only show where to aim. The dot sits a little to one side of the sensor's own view, so at short distance the spot is slightly offset from the dot.

05 Install the firmware

  1. Put MicroPython on the PicoHold the BOOTSEL button while plugging in the USB cable. A drive called RPI-RP2 appears. Download the Raspberry Pi Pico UF2 from micropython.org and drop it on that drive. It restarts by itself. This is done once.
  2. Copy main.py to the PicoDownload main.py. In Thonny (thonny.org, set the interpreter to MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico)), open the file and use File → Save as → Raspberry Pi Pico, naming it main.py. Unplug and plug the Pico back in: it starts by itself every time it gets power.
  3. Check itOpen Thonny's shell, or any serial terminal on the Pico's port. You should see a line like # hCRI temp logger 2026.11.01.1 sensors=111 emis=1.00,1.00,1.00 and then a line of numbers every second. sensors=111 means all three were found; a 0 marks one that is missing.

06 What it sends

One text line a second:

T,12.0,24.31,25.02,26.10,23.50,23.60,23.40

FieldMeaning
TMarks a reading. Lines starting with # are messages.
12.0Seconds since the Pico started.
next threeObject temperature of sensors 1, 2, 3 in °C (after the emissivity setting).
last threeTemperature of each sensor's own chip in °C (ambient at the sensor).

An empty field means that sensor did not answer. Commands you can type back: ? shows the status line, and E 2 0.9 sets sensor 2's emissivity to 0.9.

07 Emissivity

An infrared thermometer assumes the surface is a perfect radiator (emissivity 1). Anodized aluminum, plastic and paint are lower, and bare shiny metal is much lower, so the raw number reads too cold. Set each sensor's emissivity with the E command; the Pico remembers it and corrects the reading, using the sensor's own temperature for the light reflected off the surface. Typical values: anodized or painted aluminum about 0.85 to 0.95, black plastic about 0.95, bare polished metal 0.1 to 0.3 (these read poorly; put a bit of matte tape on the spot and measure that).

08 Status and next steps

  • Done: the Pico firmware and this build guide. The firmware's checksum, conversion and emissivity maths are tested on a computer; it has not been run on the hardware yet.
  • Web logger: open the logger in Chrome or Edge. It connects to the Pico over USB, shows each sensor and a live chart, records to a CSV file, lets you name the sensors and set each one's emissivity, and has a Simulate button to try it with no hardware.
  • Next: a Python logger; upload and live link on hCRI.io (Runtime and Live station); then a Companion app screen.

09 Troubleshooting

SymptomTry
sensors=000Check 3.3 V, GND, SDA (GP4) and SCL (GP5) to the multiplexer, and that the multiplexer's address pins are open (address 0x70).
One sensor is empty, the others workCheck that sensor's SDA/SCL are on the matching SDn/SCn pair, and its cable.
Readings jump or read -273Shorten the cable, and keep it away from the laser and USB wires.
The Pico does not start by itselfThe file must be named exactly main.py on the Pico.
Reads far too cold on metalSee Emissivity: shiny metal needs tape or a coating.
